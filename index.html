BallHawk Mechanical Design

I designed the chassis and drivetrain components for BallHawk, a team-built autonomous soccer training robot that could collect and launch balls, place cones, and move around the field. My work focused on creating the structure that allowed those independently developed systems to fit together in one coordinated SolidWorks assembly.

Designing around several interacting subsystems

BallHawk was designed as more than a mobile platform. The complete robot had to support a drivetrain, ball collection mechanism, launcher, cone pickup arm, sensors, and auxiliary hardware within one coordinated mechanical assembly.

That made packaging one of the main design challenges. The chassis needed to provide enough space for each mechanism while still giving independently developed subassemblies workable interfaces and preventing interference between components.

Turning the chassis concept into a complete CAD model

I developed the main body shell in SolidWorks around the larger system layout. The chassis was not just an exterior enclosure; its geometry had to organize the internal volume and provide the space and interfaces needed for the drivetrain and the other mechanisms.

The final chassis incorporated the overall vehicle envelope, curved upper shell, drivetrain openings, and the structural regions required to support the rest of the mechanical assembly.

Developing the drivetrain as a separate mechanical subassembly

I modeled the drivetrain as its own subassembly before integrating it into the chassis. This made it easier to check the wheel geometry, axle placement, and suspension relationships independently before the drivetrain was inserted into the full vehicle.

Designing the smaller interfaces that make the assembly work

Some of the most important geometry was much smaller than the chassis itself. I also designed components such as the motor mounting bracket, where hole placement, dimensions, and alignment directly affected whether the drivetrain could be assembled as intended.

These parts required thinking about the interfaces between motors, supports, axles, and the larger drivetrain structure rather than treating each component as an isolated model.

Making independently designed parts fit together

A major part of the project was making sure components created by different team members could actually assemble into one machine. We repeatedly compared dimensions and updated geometry so each subsystem had a workable location on the chassis.

For my subsystem, this meant confirming that the drivetrain geometry aligned with the body-shell openings and available internal space before the complete assembly was built.

Building the complete BallHawk assembly

Once the individual subsystems were complete, the team combined them into the final SolidWorks assembly. The chassis became the common structure around which the drivetrain, launcher, cone arm, collection ramp, sensor, compressor, battery hardware, service panel, and other auxiliary components were positioned.

The complete model was a team result. Each team member developed a separate subsystem and then integrated it into the shared assembly after the main dimensions and interfaces had been coordinated.

From subsystem design to the final machine

By the end of the project, my original chassis and drivetrain concepts had become part of a much larger coordinated mechanical assembly. The main challenge was not modeling any single component, it was making the different pieces agree on dimensions, interfaces, and available space so the overall system could be assembled coherently.

Takeaway: BallHawk gave me experience designing mechanical components as part of a larger system rather than as isolated CAD models. My chassis and drivetrain had to provide usable interfaces for several independently developed mechanisms, which made dimensional coordination, assembly planning, and component integration just as important as the individual part geometry.

Combustion CFD Research

I developed a high fidelity reacting LES model through a staged CFD workflow. I began with industry provided baseline files, established a stable FGM solution, carried that solution into a nonreacting LES, and then used the developed flowfield to build the final reacting case.

MESH SCALE19M cells
CASE CAMPAIGN8 cases
REACTING RUN20 steps
TIME STEP1 × 10−7 s

01 · CASE DEVELOPMENT STRATEGY

Building the model one stage at a time

Rather than starting with the most expensive case, I increased the model fidelity in steps. I reviewed the provided baseline files, rebuilt the chemistry and operating setup in ANSYS Fluent 2025 R2, and stabilized the FGM solution before moving to LES.

This produced a developed nonreacting flow and mixing field that I could carry directly into the reacting case. Using that solution as the starting point preserved the resolved flow structures while reaction was introduced through the FGM progress variable.

02 · DOMAIN + MESH

Resolving the injector and combustor flowfield

I worked with an approximately 19 million cell combustor mesh and checked the domain, zone assignments, connectivity, and local resolution before configuring combustion. The mesh concentrates resolution around the small fuel injection passage and its intersection with the larger chamber, where steep velocity, mixture, and thermal gradients drive the downstream solution.

03 · PHYSICS + SOLUTION SETUP

Bringing the chemistry and turbulence models together

I configured hydrogen–air chemistry from the supplied CHEMKIN mechanism and generated the flamelet/PDF data required by Fluent's partially premixed FGM formulation. The operating setup used 627 K inlet streams and an operating pressure of 1,378,950 Pa. Along the way, I corrected model, material, boundary condition, and file dependency issues that kept the inherited cases from providing a reliable starting point.

Once the FGM case was stable, I changed the turbulence model from SST k–ω to LES with the WALE subgrid scale model and used a transient formulation. This gave the LES a developed starting field instead of asking the flow, turbulence, and chemistry models to settle at the same time.

04 · NONREACTING LES BASELINE

Developing the flowfield before turning on reaction

The nonreacting FGM–LES case was the direct starting point for the final simulation. It allowed the transient jet and chamber flow to develop while the progress variable remained zero. Keeping reaction off during this stage separated flow development from combustion initialization and gave me a clear baseline for comparison.

05 · FULLY CONVERGED REACTING LES

Advancing the final reacting solution

I built the final reacting case directly from the nonreacting LES solution instead of reinitializing the domain. After setting the combustor reaction state through the FGM progress variable, I advanced the simulation for 20 time steps at 1 × 10−7 s per step to obtain the fully converged reacting LES result.

Across the eight cases, I compared temperature, pressure, velocity, and species behavior. I also reviewed three combustor cases with ANSYS engineers, which led to four model and setup corrections. Together, those changes improved model fidelity by 18% and created a more dependable baseline for later analysis.

Takeaway: The final result came from a traceable case development process that carried the chemistry, flow history, and model setup from the industry baseline through FGM, LES, and the final reacting solution.

Stellar Flare Activity of the Hyades Cluster

I analyzed light curves from the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) for stars in the Hyades open cluster to investigate how stellar flare activity varies with stellar properties and observation cadence. Building on an existing flare detection framework, I characterized flare strengths and constructed cumulative flare frequency distributions across Gaia BP-RP color ranges and TESS cadences.

Extending flare analysis to an older stellar population

Previous work in the research group focused on Nearby Young Moving Groups spanning much younger stellar ages. I extended that analysis framework to the Hyades, an open cluster at approximately 625 million years, to examine magnetic activity at a later stage of stellar evolution.

Because the stars in the Hyades are approximately the same age, the cluster also provides a useful way to compare flare behavior across stellar mass and type without introducing a large age difference between the stars.

Turning TESS light curves into comparable flare measurements

I worked with light curves from the TESS Quick-Look Pipeline and used a Python-based detection and fitting workflow to identify flare candidates. Candidate events were selected from detrended light curves and fit to recover their amplitudes and durations, with recovered flares required to rise more than 3σ above the quiescent flux level.

I then calculated fractional flare energy by integrating the flare signal over time and normalizing it by the star's quiescent brightness. Using a dimensionless energy ratio made it possible to compare flare strengths across stars with different baseline brightness levels.

Building the flare frequency distribution for the cluster

I combined the recovered events into a cumulative flare frequency distribution (FFD). The horizontal axis represents fractional flare energy, while the vertical axis shows the fraction of flares stronger than a given energy, allowing the overall activity distribution of the cluster to be viewed in a single curve.

2308 flares represented in the cumulative Hyades FFD

Testing how observation cadence changes the result

TESS does not observe every target at the same time resolution, so I compared the flare distributions from the 10-minute and 200-second cadence samples separately. The higher cadence dataset contained substantially more detected events and better captured short-duration flares.

531 flares in the 10-minute cadence FFD
1764 flares in the 200-second cadence FFD

This comparison showed that observation cadence is not just a plotting choice; it changes which flare population can be recovered from the data and therefore affects the activity trends inferred from the light curves.

Comparing activity across stellar types

Gaia BP-RP color provides a proxy for stellar temperature and spectral type. I separated each cadence sample into broad BP-RP ranges and compared their FFDs to look for differences in flare behavior across hotter and cooler stars.

The preliminary distributions suggested that cooler, lower mass stars at higher BP-RP values remained more flare active than the bluer stellar populations. I treated this as a trend to investigate rather than a final conclusion because the cadence groups and stellar-color bins contain different sample sizes.

Looking beyond flare frequency

I also compared equivalent flare duration with fractional flare energy for the two cadence groups. Plotting the individual events alongside their distributions provided another view of how temporal resolution changes the flare population recovered from the observations.

What the analysis showed

The Hyades analysis showed how both stellar characteristics and observational choices affect the activity patterns extracted from astronomical time series data. Shorter cadence observations recovered a larger population of short flares, while the BP-RP comparisons revealed preliminary differences in activity across stellar types.

Extending the analysis to the older Hyades population also created a bridge between the younger stellar groups studied by the existing pipeline and later stages of magnetic evolution.

Takeaway: This project gave me experience turning astronomical time series data into interpretable physical trends. I worked across light curve processing, Python-based flare fitting, energy normalization, and population-level visualization while learning how observational factors such as cadence can affect the conclusions drawn from a dataset.

Urban Drone Delivery System MBSE

I developed a complete SysML model of an autonomous package delivery drone, connecting its urban operating context to requirements, system architecture, mission behavior, performance equations, and scenario level feasibility checks. The result was an integrated model that could explain both how the system was organized and whether a proposed mission should proceed.

RANGE TARGET≥ 10 km
BATTERY TARGET≥ 40 min
PAYLOAD LIMIT≤ 2 kg
TRADE CASES3 scenarios

01 · SYSTEM BOUNDARY + REQUIREMENTS

Defining the mission before defining the vehicle

I began by setting the system boundary around an urban delivery mission. The drone receives a request from the customer, accepts commands from a ground station, operates within wind, obstacle, and no-fly-zone constraints, and delivers a package to a specified location. Establishing those external interactions prevented the model from treating the aircraft as an isolated product.

The top level requirement, to deliver packages autonomously and safely, was decomposed into performance, energy, safety, and payload branches. This created traceable targets for delivery time, range, speed, battery life, power consumption, obstacle avoidance, emergency landing, and payload capacity.

02 · LOGICAL ARCHITECTURE

Decomposing the system into accountable subsystems

I used a block definition diagram to organize the system hierarchically. The drone contains the battery, propulsion system, motor, flight controller, sensors, frame, navigation, communications, payload handling, and obstacle avoidance functions. The ground station was separately decomposed into mission planning, telemetry, operator interface, and communication elements.

Keeping the vehicle, ground segment, environment, and destination distinct made ownership of each value and constraint clearer. It also created a consistent structure for connecting later behavior and parametric analysis back to the responsible blocks.

03 · NOMINAL + OFF-NOMINAL BEHAVIOR

Modeling how the mission proceeds and when it stops

The activity model describes the full delivery workflow: receive the request, validate payload and energy, plan the route, take off, navigate, respond to obstacles, confirm a safe landing area, deliver the package, and return to base. Decision nodes turn safety requirements into go/no-go logic.

I complemented the process view with a state machine for discrete operating modes. Cruise can transition into obstacle avoidance or hold position, while unsafe conditions route the vehicle toward fault handling. Together, the two diagrams capture both the sequence of mission activities and the vehicle state at each point.

04 · EXECUTABLE PARAMETRICS

Connecting architecture to quantitative performance

I embedded constraint blocks for battery life, delivery time, range, power, and payload. Battery capacity and power consumption determine endurance, distance and speed determine delivery time, and speed and endurance determine range. Binding these relationships to block values turned the model from a static description into an analysis framework.

05 · TRADE STUDY + FEASIBILITY

Using scenarios to reveal mission level consequences

I evaluated baseline, heavy payload, and long distance scenarios while holding battery capacity, power consumption, and speed constant. The baseline case produced an 11.1 minute delivery time, 48 minutes of battery life, and 43 km of modeled range. Doubling the route distance increased delivery time to 22.2 minutes while endurance and range remained fixed, as expected from the governing inputs.

BASELINE DELIVERY11.1 min
LONG-DISTANCE CASE22.2 min
MODELED ENDURANCE48 min
HEAVY PAYLOADMission rejected

Takeaway: The project showed how MBSE can preserve a continuous thread from stakeholder context and requirements to architecture, behavior, equations, and mission decisions. The 3 kg payload case was rejected against the 2 kg limit, making requirement compliance an active part of the model.

CAD Benchmarking & AI Model Evaluation

I helped develop and review complex CAD benchmarks used to assess AI-generated engineering work. The role brought together mechanical design, requirements interpretation, rubric development, and CAD validation. I checked each submission for geometric accuracy, completeness, and consistency with the original design brief.

Turning design requirements into clear CAD tasks

A strong CAD benchmark needs to be detailed enough for different reviewers to reach the same conclusion. I translated engineering instructions into focused tasks that clearly defined the expected geometry, features, assembly relationships, and final deliverables.

The key challenge was making every requirement measurable. A model could not pass simply because it resembled the reference; the task also needed to confirm that the correct components, features, and mechanical relationships were present.

Comparing the design intent with the final model

For each benchmark, I compared the submitted CAD work with both the written brief and the reference geometry. I began with the overall mechanism, then worked through the smaller details that determined whether the design was truly accurate.

For the claw gripper shown here, I reviewed the opposing jaws, gear train, linkages, pivot locations, base geometry, and mounting features, along with how those parts came together as a complete mechanical assembly.

Building an objective evaluation rubric

Rather than assigning one subjective score to an entire model, I broke each CAD task into a series of focused checks. The rubric covered elements such as required components, feature construction, gear alignment, linkage connections, mounting geometry, and assembly organization.

This approach made reviews more consistent and repeatable. A submission might look convincing at first glance but still miss an important feature, interface, or mechanical relationship.

Looking beyond visual similarity

The final review went beyond screenshots. I opened the CAD model and inspected its dimensions, feature placement, component relationships, and overall compliance with the rubric.

Direct inspection was especially important for details that might appear correct in an isometric view while being wrong in the underlying model, including spacing, alignment, hole placement, and clearances between moving parts.

Approaching CAD as both a designer and a reviewer

This work required me to think about CAD from two perspectives: how an engineer would build the model and how a reviewer could verify the result. It reinforced that clear instructions and measurable acceptance criteria are just as important as the final geometry.

Takeaway: This project strengthened my ability to turn engineering requirements into clear CAD tasks, build objective rubrics, and verify models for accuracy and completeness. It also taught me to evaluate the design information behind a model instead of relying on appearance alone.

Unified Aerodynamic Analysis Tool

I built a MATLAB solver to analyze a two dimensional double wedge airfoil from subsonic through supersonic conditions. The tool chooses the appropriate aerodynamic model, calculates pressure and force coefficients, runs parametric studies, and searches for the most efficient valid design.

MACH1.52
ANGLE OF ATTACK2°
WEDGE HALF-ANGLE2°
MAXIMUM L/D14.306

01 · TOOL ARCHITECTURE

Using one workflow across multiple flow regimes

Aerodynamic models are only reliable within specific operating ranges, so I designed the solver around model validity rather than forcing one equation set across every Mach number. The user enters freestream Mach number, angle of attack, and wedge half angle, and the code validates those inputs and routes the case to the suitable analysis method.

SUBSONIC · M < 0.8

Linearized thin airfoil theory with a Prandtl–Glauert compressibility correction.

TRANSONIC · 0.8–1.2

The solver stops instead of returning results from a model outside its valid range.

SUPERSONIC · M > 1.2

Panel-by-panel shock expansion theory using oblique shocks and Prandtl–Meyer expansions.

02 · MODEL COMPARISON

Capturing different aerodynamic behavior below and above Mach 1

In the subsonic model, lift varied linearly with angle of attack and increased as the Prandtl–Glauert correction grew stronger. At Mach 0.3, the lift coefficient changed from −0.6897 at −6° to 1.0346 at 9°. The supersonic solution remained linear with angle of attack, but its lower slope reflected the different pressure response predicted by shock expansion theory.

03 · SUPERSONIC PANEL SOLUTION

Marching shocks and expansions across the airfoil

For supersonic cases, I followed the upper and lower surfaces independently from leading edge to trailing edge. Each turn was classified as a compression or expansion. Compression corners used the weak attached solution of the θ–β–M relation, while expansion corners used the Prandtl–Meyer function. The state leaving each front panel became the starting condition for its rear panel.

At Mach 2, a 2° angle of attack, and a 3° wedge half angle, the lower front panel produced the strongest compression and reached a pressure coefficient of 0.11265. The rear panel expansions reduced pressure on both surfaces before the panel forces were integrated into lift and inviscid wave drag.

04 · PARAMETRIC STUDY

Finding the design variable that controlled efficiency

I swept Mach number, angle of attack, and wedge half angle to separate their effects. Wedge angle produced the clearest design tradeoff. At Mach 2 and 2° angle of attack, increasing the half-angle from 2° to 10° raised lift coefficient only from 0.08082 to 0.08545, while wave-drag coefficient increased from 0.00565 to 0.07604.

The result showed that stronger flow turning added wave drag much faster than lift. Aerodynamic efficiency therefore fell from an L/D of 14.3 to 1.12 across the sweep, making thickness the dominant variable in the final design search.

05 · DESIGN SEARCH

Optimizing within the limits of the aerodynamic model

The final search covered Mach 1.3–3.0, angles of attack from 0.5°–10°, and wedge half angles from 2°–10°. Cases with detached shocks were rejected automatically, and a finer search was run around the best initial result. The optimum occurred at Mach 1.52, α = 2°, and δ = 2°, producing CL = 0.122831, CD = 0.008586, and L/D = 14.306.

Takeaway: This project strengthened my ability to turn aerodynamic theory into a reusable computational tool, recognize where each model is valid, and connect parametric results to a defensible engineering design decision.

ADDITIONAL PROJECT · COMPRESSIBLE FLOW ×

Reduced-Scale Wind Tunnel Design

I developed a preliminary design analysis for a two dimensional, closed circuit supersonic wind tunnel. I used MATLAB to compare air, argon, and carbon dioxide across Mach 1.5–4.5 and stagnation temperatures from 300–700 K. The goal was to determine how those choices would affect the tunnel's physical dimensions, startup power, operating temperature, and the pressure and thermal conditions around a test model.

MACH RANGE1.5–4.5
STAGNATION TEMPERATURE300–700 K
WORKING FLUIDS3
MODEL APEX ANGLE20°

01 · DESIGN QUESTION

Connecting tunnel operation to the test environment

The design brief called for a closed circuit tunnel that could produce supersonic test conditions over a wide operating range. I needed to evaluate three possible gases (air, argon, and carbon dioxide) at test-section Mach numbers from 1.5 to 4.5 and inlet stagnation temperatures from 300 to 700 K. Each choice changed the tunnel geometry, the energy required to start it, and the environment surrounding a test model.

I therefore split the work into two linked problems. First, I sized the converging-diverging nozzle and diffuser throats, estimated maximum compressor power during startup, and calculated the static temperature entering the test section. Then I placed a symmetric 20° wedge in the modeled flow and predicted its oblique shock angle, surface pressure ratio, and downstream temperature. Together, these calculations showed both what the tunnel would require to operate and what an experiment inside it would experience.

TUNNEL OPERATION

Isentropic area relations, normal shock losses, throat sizing, compressor power, and static temperature.

MODEL FLOWFIELD

Numerical θ–β–M solution, surface pressure ratio, and downstream shock temperature.

02 · NOZZLE AND DIFFUSER SIZING

Translating Mach number into tunnel geometry

For steady operation, the converging-diverging nozzle accelerates the gas to the target test section Mach number. Because the tunnel was modeled with a constant span, the compressible area relation could be written directly as a height ratio. As Mach number increased, the required nozzle throat became much smaller relative to the test section.

I also sized the diffuser throat around the minimum loss starting condition, with the normal shock positioned at the second throat. This requirement moved in the opposite direction, as the second to first throat ratio rose rapidly with Mach number. Argon produced the smallest geometry change at the high end of the design space, while carbon dioxide required the largest.

03 · OPERATING COST AND THERMAL LIMITS

Estimating what it takes to start and run the tunnel

I used conservation of energy, compressible mass flow, and stagnation property relations to estimate normalized maximum compressor power. The power demand stayed relatively modest through much of the range, then climbed sharply above roughly Mach 3.5. Higher stagnation temperature increased that demand, and carbon dioxide was the most power intensive of the three fluids. Argon required substantially less.

The easier startup came with a thermal tradeoff. Isentropic expansion through the nozzle reduced the test section static temperature as Mach number increased. Argon produced the lowest temperatures, while carbon dioxide remained warmer. This meant the working fluid could not be selected from geometry or power alone, material limits and the desired test environment also mattered.

04 · WEDGE FLOWFIELD

Predicting the loads and temperatures around a test model

To connect the facility design with an actual experiment, I analyzed a symmetric wedge with a 20° apex angle. The MATLAB tool solved the θ–β–M equation numerically for the weak oblique shock, then used the normal Mach component to calculate the pressure rise and temperature change across the shock.

The shock swept closer to the model as Mach number increased. Argon consistently produced the steepest shock and the largest surface pressure ratio, which would make it useful for higher load testing but would also increase the risk of shock reflections from the test section walls.

05 · DESIGN TRADEOFF

Choosing a gas based on the mission of the facility

Downstream temperature revealed the clearest limitation of argon. Although every gas heated as it crossed the oblique shock, the upstream isentropic expansion dominated the overall trend. At a 300 K stagnation temperature, argon fell to about 65 K near Mach 4.5, compared with warmer conditions for air and carbon dioxide.

Takeaway: There was no single best working fluid. Argon reduced startup power and eased geometric sizing, but it created the coldest and most severe model environment. Air and carbon dioxide required more demanding tunnel operation but offered warmer test conditions. The final choice depends on whether facility efficiency or a particular aerodynamic test environment is the stronger priority.

Wind Turbine Blade Fluid-Structure Interaction Analysis

I completed a steady state, one way FSI analysis in ANSYS, following the load path from the surrounding airflow to the pressure on the blade and finally to the blade's structural response.

TOOLS

ANSYS Fluent · ANSYS Mechanical · ANSYS CFD-Post

STRUCTURE

Orthotropic composite shell + internal spar

The problem: connect the airflow to the structure

The project follows a roughly 43 meter utility scale wind turbine blade through a complete CFD and FEA workflow. The blade transitions from a cylindrical root into S818, S825, and S826 airfoil sections, with spanwise twist and a 4° pitch angle at the tip.

Instead of treating aerodynamics and structures as unrelated analyses, the goal was to calculate the pressure field in Fluent and transfer that same loading into Mechanical. Because the structural deformation is not sent back to the CFD model, this is a steady state, one way fluid structure interaction analysis.

Modeling a rotating blade without a moving mesh

The incoming wind speed is 12 m/s and the blade rotates at 2.22 rad/s, giving a tip-speed ratio of approximately 8. I modeled the flow in a rotating reference frame, which captures the blade rotation while allowing the computational mesh itself to remain stationary.

The CFD model uses the Reynolds-averaged continuity and Navier-Stokes equations with the SST k-ω turbulence model. The inlet is prescribed at 12 m/s, the outlet at atmospheric pressure, the blade is a no slip wall, and the two side faces are periodic.

Solving the aerodynamic side

I solved the flow with Fluent's pressure based solver and monitored convergence before extracting the quantities needed for the next stage. The CFD solution shows how pressure changes over the blade surface and how the rotating blade redirects the surrounding flow.

CFD VERIFICATION

The simulated blade-tip velocity was 98.05 m/s compared with 98.12 m/s from the hand calculation. I also checked mass balance, pressure convergence, iteration convergence, and the effect of mesh refinement before using the aerodynamic loads downstream.

Passing the aerodynamic load into FEA

The key step in the project is the Workbench connection between the Fluent and Mechanical systems. The pressure field calculated on the wetted blade surfaces is imported directly as a structural pressure load, preserving the spatial variation found in the CFD solution.

Building the structural model

The blade is represented as an orthotropic composite structure with an outer shell and an internal spar. Both components vary in thickness along the span, reflecting the much heavier root region and thinner tip. In Mechanical, I assigned the composite material, thickness distribution, rotational velocity, root constraint, and imported aerodynamic pressure field before solving for the structural response.

I then evaluated total deformation and von-Mises equivalent stress to see where the aerodynamic and rotational loads produce the largest structural response.

Verification mattered as much as the contour plots

On the structural side, the ANSYS root radial force was 1578.1 kN compared with a 1576.3 kN hand calculation, a difference of about 0.1%. The CFD mesh-refinement study also showed that the baseline mesh was not fully mesh independent for power prediction, emphasizing the importance of verifying numerical convergence rather than relying only on visually smooth contour results.

98.05 m/s CFD blade-tip velocity
98.12 m/s hand-calculated tip velocity
0.1% root radial-force difference

Takeaway: this project gave me an end-to-end view of multiphysics simulation - defining the aerodynamic model, checking the CFD solution, transferring distributed loads, and evaluating the resulting structural response in a single connected ANSYS workflow.

Mars Opportunity Rover Mobility Subsystem Modeling & Feasibility Analysis

I translated the physical behavior of the Mars Opportunity Rover's mobility subsystem into an executable SysML model, connecting rover architecture, engineering constraints, parametric simulation, and system-level feasibility analysis.

TOOLS

MagicDraw / Cameo · ParaMagic · ModelCenter

Modeling the rover as an integrated system

The research began by representing the Opportunity Rover as a connected system rather than a collection of independent components. The team model organized the rover into six major subsystems: mobility, power, structure, instrumentation, guidance/navigation/control, and thermal control.

Each subsystem contained its own parameters and relationships, while the full SysML architecture established how those subsystem-level quantities contributed to the behavior of the complete rover.

My responsibility: the Mobility subsystem

I was responsible for the rover's mobility subsystem. I developed the mobility block in MagicDraw, assigned engineering value properties and units, and modeled the relationships needed to determine whether the rover could move across Martian terrain while satisfying torque, traction, speed, power, and climbing constraints.

TORQUE T = F r

Relates wheel traction force to required motor torque.

TRACTION Fmax = μmg cos(θ)

Estimates the maximum wheel-soil traction available on a slope.

SPEED v = ωr

Converts wheel angular velocity into rover translational speed.

POWER P = ηT(v/r)

Connects torque and speed to the mobility power requirement.

CLIMB LIMIT θmax = atan(μ)

Estimates the steepest terrain supported by the available friction.

Turning the equations into an executable parametric model

Defining the constraint equations was only part of the model. I also had to bind the correct Mobility value properties to the inputs and outputs of each constraint so information could propagate through the subsystem.

Shared variables such as wheel radius, coefficient of friction, angular velocity, traction force, and motor torque were connected across multiple relationships. The resulting parametric diagram turned the subsystem into a connected model instead of a set of isolated equations.

Executing the model with ParaMagic

After completing the parameter bindings, I executed the subsystem using ParaMagic. Assigned input values propagated through the constraint network, allowing the model to calculate mobility performance directly from the connected SysML relationships.

REPRESENTATIVE OUTPUTS

Payload-Integrated Rocket Design & Flight Validation

For a team-built model rocket, I designed and revised the 3D-printed bay that carried a PerfectFlite Pnut altimeter. I also helped manufacture the vehicle and used the flight test to compare the finished rocket with our preflight performance model.

MISSION

Carry an altimeter to a target altitude of 125 ft while maintaining stable flight and safe recovery.

Designing around a mission requirement

The project began with a specific objective: modify an Estes Viking model rocket to reach approximately 125 ft while carrying a PerfectFlite Pnut altimeter. The payload had to remain protected from engine heat and landing loads while still allowing the altimeter to measure atmospheric pressure during flight.

That requirement turned the project into a higher level design problem. Added payload mass affected altitude and stability, the payload bay had to integrate with the nosecone and recovery system, and the vehicle still had to be light enough to approach the target altitude.

My responsibility: integrating the altimeter payload

My main design responsibility was the payload bay. I created and revised the CAD model used to house the altimeter between the nosecone and main body tube, and I also helped manufacture the final rocket.

The bay incorporated pressure equalization holes so the altimeter could sense atmospheric pressure during ascent. Internal stops prevented the instrument from sliding into the nosecone or body tube, while the overall geometry had to allow quick installation before launch and isolate the electronics from the motor and recovery system below it.

Checking stability and predicted flight performance

Because the payload and bay changed the mass distribution of the rocket, our team used OpenRocket to evaluate the full vehicle before flight. The model supported decisions about payload placement, engine selection, stability, and predicted altitude.

The final modeled configuration had a mass of 55.3 g with the motor, an overall length of 57.7 cm, and placed the center of gravity ahead of the center of pressure. OpenRocket predicted an apogee of 151.6 ft for the selected A8-3 motor.

55.3 g modeled mass with motor
57.7 cm overall rocket length
151.6 ft OpenRocket predicted apogee

Iterating the design for manufacturing

The original concept included both a 3D printed payload bay and a printed nosecone. After dimensional and weight concerns appeared, our team kept the lighter nosecone from the kit and printed only the payload bay in PETG, selected for its strength and heat resistance relative to PLA.

I revised the payload bay geometry and fit as the design developed. The team also replaced the kit's cardboard fins with laser-cut balsa fins for greater structural support. Two fins later cracked during transportation and required tape reinforcement, adding unplanned mass and surface irregularity to the final vehicle.

Flight testing the completed vehicle

After launch, the vehicle followed a straight, stable ascent and the onboard altimeter recorded a maximum altitude of 119 ft, only 6 ft below the 125 ft target.

125 ft target altitude
151.6 ft OpenRocket prediction
119 ft measured flight apogee
TEST RESULT

The rocket met the main altitude objective closely, but the flight also exposed behavior that was not fully represented in the preflight model, making the test useful for understanding differences between simulation and hardware.

What the flight test exposed

Near apogee, the motor was ejected from the bottom of the rocket and the streamer recovery system did not deploy successfully. We identified the motor's deployment behavior and the tight payload bay/body tube fit as possible factors in the recovery system failure.

The measured 119 ft apogee was also well below the 151.6 ft OpenRocket prediction. Differences between the modeled and manufactured vehicle, including actual mass, fin alignment, surface changes, and recovery assumptions, highlighted the importance of verifying the simulation with hardware.

Closing the design-to-test loop

The clearest improvement would be to update the simulation using the final manufactured mass and dimensions, then redesign the payload/recovery interface so that the bay remains secure without restricting separation or streamer deployment. A more durable fin design would also reduce the need for reinforcement that changes the vehicle's mass and surface.

Takeaway: this project connected CAD design with hardware and flight validation. Designing the payload bay was only one part of the challenge; the test showed how tolerances, mass, manufacturing variation, recovery system integration, and simulation assumptions all influence the performance of the full system.
